# Chicago potholes

[Explore the project](https://connorblandford.com/projects/chicago-potholes/). This is the dataset behind an exploratory map; the accompanying article is forthcoming. ## How this data is structured, and why

The shape is meant to make the file hard to misread rather than quick to skim. Four decisions do most of that work.

**One long table, not a spreadsheet per topic.** Every figure in `potholes_long.csv` sits on its own row keyed by `(geo_type, geo_id, period, metric)`. Filter to the metric and period you want and you have a tidy table of places; no column means a different thing in a different year, and adding a year or a measure adds rows rather than reshaping anything you have already written against. The cost is that you filter before you chart. That is deliberate — a wide file invites people to compare two columns that were never comparable.

**The count behind every number travels with it.** `n` is published on every row, *including rows where the value is blank*. A rate of 12 per street mile means something different behind 40 reports than behind 900, and a figure withheld for thinness should still tell you how thin it was. Nothing here asks you to take a number on faith about its own weight.

**Caveats are data, not a footnote.** `flags` is a pipe-delimited column on every row, and the conditions that make a figure hard to read — a partial year, a ward period before the 2023 remap, a cohort still open, a crew entry of 150+ potholes on one block — are attached to the exact rows they apply to, including every row that draws on such a period (a comparison whose earlier window predates the remap carries the remap flag). A caveat written in prose gets separated from the number the first time someone copies a cell. A caveat in a column does not. **Never read a value without reading its flags.**

**Thin cells are blank, and blank on a published rule.** A figure is withheld when fewer than 30 records sit behind it (100 for a 90th percentile). For rates the test uses the *expected* count for a place of that size, not the observed one. That distinction matters: if suppression keyed off the observed count, genuinely quiet areas would vanish from the map and the map would systematically overstate how much of the city has a pothole problem. Under an expected-count rule, a quiet area shows up as quiet. Every rule is re-derived from the published `n` as a validation check (V8).

Two smaller things follow from the same intent. Color-class boundaries in `breaks.json` are **frozen** at the methods version, so adding a year does not silently recolor earlier years. And every `value` in `potholes_long.csv` arrives **pre-rounded** to a fixed precision per measure, so the file and the map cannot disagree about what a number is. That rule covers the primary file only: the companion tables carry the precision they were computed at, so `strata_tracts.csv`'s `poverty_rate` runs to full floating-point precision and `geo_denominators.csv`'s `street_mi` to four decimals.

## Sources and method

Inputs are the City of Chicago datasets `v6vf-nfxy` (311 service requests, pothole codes PHF for streets and PHB for alleys), `wqdh-9gek` (Potholes Patched), `p293-wvbd` (wards, 2023–), `k9yb-bpqx` (wards 2015–2023, used for the overlay and for quality checks only), `igwz-8jzy` (community areas) and `pr57-gg9e` (street centerlines); the 2020 Census P.L. 94-171 tables, the ACS 5-year tract tables and TIGER/Line 2020 geometry from the Census Bureau; and, for the winter score only, NOAA's Global Historical Climatology Network–Daily records for O'Hare (`USW00094846`) and Midway (`USW00014819`), from the NCEI Access Data Service. Raw snapshots are preserved privately; every published figure is reproducible from the dataset IDs and parameters recorded in [METHODS.md](METHODS.md) §1.

Patched records are linked back to the 311 request that prompted them. Records are placed by point-in-polygon on their own coordinates, **not** by the ward or community-area field 311 stored when the request was filed — those reflect assignment at creation time and disagree with the boundaries on about 4% of pre-remap rows. Per-mile rates use centerline mileage with boundary segments split between the places that share them, so street miles sum to the city total. **They exclude expressways, the Skyway and ramps, but not Lake Shore Drive:** enough fills are recorded on it (0.6% of all fills) that it is kept in both the counts and the mileage.

Response times are estimated with a Kaplan–Meier survival curve rather than by averaging closed reports. Reports still open on the snapshot date are counted as open rather than dropped, which is what stops a recent year looking artificially fast because its slow cases have not finished yet. **This is also why there is no mean days-to-close anywhere in the release:** a mean cannot be computed from censored data without inventing closing dates for reports that have not closed. The published figures are medians and 90th percentiles from the survival curve.

"Larger than normal" on a change measure means larger than areas actually vary, not larger than counting chance alone: each layer's spread is measured (a dispersion factor, published in `change_summary.csv`), and the areas that stand out are held to a 5% false discovery rate across all the areas tested at once. Few areas pass. These comparisons are in the data only — the map draws the four Level layers, not a Change layer.

[METHODS.md](METHODS.md) defines every number — filters, linkage, geography, the estimator, suppression, class breaks and the validation gates — and lists the literature it draws on. If the code and that document disagree, the code is wrong.

**What changed in 0.5.** Two validation thresholds that had been loosened between 0.3 and 0.4 so the build would pass were restored, and their failures are now published rather than hidden: geography loss is back to a 1.5% cap and the snap-coverage diagnostic back to a 97% target. Checks that had never run — the reproducibility check, the survival-curve check and several warnings — now run in every build. Suppression is stricter for 90th percentiles, the change test now allows for how much areas really vary, 2019 has one published policy, and the equity strata gained their own street miles and population. [METHODS.md §13](METHODS.md#13-changelog) is the full changelog, entry by entry. On 2026-09-23 the release gained `facts.json` and the two summary tables, derived from the tables already published; no figure changed, and every other file is byte-identical to the 0.5 release of 2026-09-22. On 2026-09-24 the release gained V7b's open-share threshold in `facts.json` (`km_open_share_pct`, 0.5%) and began publishing the map's own files under `site/`, listed in their own `site/checksums.sha256`. It is built from the same 2026-09-21 snapshot and no figure changed: every data file except `facts.json` and `qa_report.json` is byte-identical to the release of 2026-09-23, and `qa_report.json` differs only in the reproducibility check's file count. Validation was not rerun, because one of its checks (V1c) compares the snapshot against the live City portal, which has moved on since 2026-09-21; the published results are that snapshot's.

## Before reporting

- **These are not counts of potholes.** Nothing in this release measures how many potholes exist or the condition of any street. Reports measure attention; fills measure work. Neither measures pavement.
- **More reports per street mile is not more potholes per street mile.** Weather, awareness, app use and ward-office practice all move the reporting number. A quiet area may be a well-paved one or one that does not call.
- **A closed report is not a patched pothole.** Closure is an administrative status. Only a linked Potholes Patched record indicates a recorded patch; `rpt_patched_share` is how often that happened — 71.2% of completed 2025 reports, 76.7% in 2024.
- **"Time to patch" is time to close, for the reports that got a patch record.** For 99.95% of linked pairs the patch completion is recorded within a minute of the 311 closure: the two datasets log one event. So `patch_p50_d` and `patch_p90_d` are not a separate clock. A difference from `close_p50_d` reflects which reports got a patch record, not a wait between closing and patching.
- **2019 is published but enters no comparison.** It was the first full year of the current 311 system, and 2.09% of its reports could not be placed in a community area at all (891 of 42,535), against 0.37%–0.78% in every later full year; 46.8% of its in-demand requests carry a duplicate flag, the highest share in the series. Its rows are here, flagged `TRANSITION_2019`, because excluding data is not the same as hiding it. It is in no pooled period (the equity pool is `CY2020-2025`), no year-over-year figure (2020's is blank), no change comparison, no color class and no diagnostic headline, and the website's map and charts start at 2020. Treat it as a weak baseline, not as a comparable year.
- **2026 is not over.** Use the `YTD{YYYY}-08` periods to compare it with earlier years; they cover January 1 – August 31 in every year and observe response times as of the same date each year. A `CY2026` row is a partial year, flagged `PARTIAL_YEAR`, and its year-over-year change is blank.
- **Fill counts are crew-entered and unverified.** One report can lead to patching a whole block, so fills routinely exceed reports — about 249,000 fills against 20,911 reports in 2025. Some entries record 150 or more potholes on one block; they are kept as recorded, and `EXTREME_FILL` marks the place-years where such entries are 10% or more of the total.
- **Patch timings are withheld for years that have not settled.** When 1% or more of a place's cohort is still open, `patch_p50_d`, `patch_p90_d` and `rpt_patched_share` are blank with the flag `COHORT_OPEN`, rather than published from the subset that happens to have finished.
- **Nothing here grades an alderperson.** Ward offices file requests; CDOT fills potholes. Ward figures before 2023-05-15 are shown on today's ward map so a place can be followed over time, and describe an area that different alderpersons represented. `ward_legacy_mix.csv` records the overlap, by number. No alderperson name appears anywhere in this release.
- **Alley figures are counts and response times only.** The city publishes no alley centerline mileage, so there is no alley rate per mile and no per-capita variant. Alley reports are also sparse enough per place that a fifth to three in ten community areas are withheld in a full year, and a quarter to a half in a year-to-date window.
- **Group comparisons are ecological.** `pov_quintile` and `race_group` rows describe census tracts, not people, and say nothing about how any individual was treated. Poverty and race are strongly collinear in Chicago; they are reported separately and are never cross-tabulated. **Majority-Asian tracts are counted under "no majority":** there are six, with about 20,900 residents and fewer reports a year than the 300 a group needs to stand alone. `strata_tracts.csv` shows each tract's group before and after that merge.
- **The winter score is context, not a cause.** It is built from weather records alone, and its rank correlation with citywide reports is not distinguishable from zero over the years available (see below). A harsh winter beside a high report count does not show that one produced the other.
- **Coverage stops at 311.** Private drives are not covered, and a pothole nobody reported is not in the data.
- **`UNASSIGNED` is a bucket, not a place.** Records whose coordinates fall outside every boundary are totaled under that id for count measures only. It is never mapped, and it should not be charted beside real places.
- **Portal datasets get revised retroactively.** The build reads a dated snapshot, so these figures stay reproducible after an upstream revision — and will differ from a query run against the live portal today.

## Validation

<!-- gen:validation -->Of the 15 build-blocking checks the methods define (V1–V15), 13 pass and 2 fail and are published rather than loosened. **V3** caps geography loss at 1.5% of each count in each year; 6 count-years exceed it: crew-logged records in 2021, 2022 and 2026, reports in 2019, staff-observed records in 2019 and reports including duplicates in 2019 (worst: crew-logged records in 2021, 2.16% on wards). **V7b** asks the survival-curve median to sit within 0.5 day of the median of closed reports alone where under 0.5% of reports are open; it misses in 11 of 2,375 cohorts, where counting the one or two open reports moves the median one report along, across a gap of more than half a day. An exact check (**V7c**) confirms the estimator in all 2,375 cohorts, those 11 included. 6 warning checks are open: V1d, the current partial year drifted between the pull and validation (−0.28% on current-year report rows); V3w, geography loss between 1% and the 1.5% cap; W3, 56 area-years where crew entries of 150+ potholes make up 10% or more of fills; W4, 125 area-years above three times their own median, mostly alley closing times (57), street closing times (33) and patch times (25); W6, a race group too thin to stand alone, merged into “no majority” (majority-Asian tracts); W9, the Midway cross-check of the O'Hare winter score (ρ = 0.75 against a 0.85 target). 2 diagnostics miss their targets: D6, where 93.69% of reports lie within 100 ft of a street centerline against a 97% target (the rest count as unsnapped); D9, where the 311 ward field disagrees with the coordinates for 4.18% of pre-remap reports against a 2% trigger (placement never uses that field; the cause is not yet investigated). The winter score's rank correlation with citywide reports is ρ = 0.2 over 2020–2025 (n = 6) and 0.5 with 2019 (n = 7); neither is distinguishable from zero (exact p = 0.71 and 0.27). Every result is in `qa_report.json`.<!-- /gen:validation -->
